01 Foundations

The logic underneath everything else: how selection builds minds, and what those minds are made of.

The three products of evolution

Adaptations

Inherited, reliably-developing features that solved a recurrent ancestral problem and were retained because they aided survival or reproduction. e.g. sweat glands, fear of snakes, taste for sugar.

Byproducts

Traits carried along by an adaptation but with no function of their own. e.g. the belly button (byproduct of the umbilical cord); the whiteness of bone.

Noise

Random variation from mutation, chance environmental effects, or genetic drift — not designed, not functional. e.g. exact navel shape.

Selection: three flavors

TypeCurrencyDrives
Natural selectionSurvivalAnti-predator, food, thermoregulation, disease avoidance.
Sexual selectionMating successTraits that win mates, even at a survival cost (peacock's tail).
Inclusive fitnessGene copiesOwn reproduction plus effects on genetic relatives (Hamilton, 1964).

Sexual selection runs through two routes: intrasexual competition (same-sex rivals fight/compete for access) and intersexual selection (one sex chooses; preferred traits spread).

Evolved psychological mechanisms (EPMs)

Buss's central unit. The mind is not one general-purpose computer but a large set of specialized programs, each shaped to solve a specific adaptive problem.

  • Domain-specific — each is activated by a narrow slice of information (cues of a snake, a rival, a fertile mate).
  • Numerous — many mechanisms, because there were many distinct problems.
  • Functional — structured as input → decision rule → output (physiology, behavior, or information to another mechanism).

Levels of explanation

An ultimate (why) explanation names the adaptive function; a proximate (how) explanation names the immediate mechanism. They're complementary, not rival. "We crave sugar because it signaled scarce calories" (ultimate) vs. "sweet receptors trigger dopamine" (proximate).

Key idea: our minds were built for the ancestral environment (the EEA — Environment of Evolutionary Adaptedness), not the modern world. Mismatch explains junk-food cravings, phobias of snakes over cars, and more.

02 The Theory Toolbox

Five mid-level theories do most of the explanatory work across the book. Learn these and most findings become predictable.

TheoryAuthorOne-line logic
Parental InvestmentTrivers 1972The sex that invests more per offspring becomes the choosier one; the lesser-investing sex competes for access.

Hamilton's rule in words: a gene for helping spreads when the benefit to the recipient, weighted by how related they are to you, outweighs the cost to yourself. Full siblings and parent–child share r = 0.5; grandparent/grandchild and half-siblings r = 0.25; first cousins r = 0.125.

03 Problems of Survival

Staying alive long enough to reproduce: food, predators, disease, and the landscapes we're drawn to.

Food & foraging

  • Universal taste preferences for sugar, fat, and salt — all rare and calorie/nutrient-dense ancestrally, now overabundant (mismatch → obesity).
  • Disgust as a disease-avoidance adaptation — the "behavioral immune system." Strongly triggered by rotten food, bodily waste, disease cues; intensifies during pregnancy's first trimester when the immune system is suppressed.
  • Pregnancy sickness plausibly protects the vulnerable embryo from toxins/teratogens (aversion peaks in the first trimester). Profet.

Fears & phobias

Human fears cluster around ancestral dangers, not modern ones. We readily learn to fear snakes, spiders, heights, darkness, strangers, deep water — but rarely cars or electrical outlets, which kill far more people today.

  • Prepared learning — snake/spider fears are acquired after minimal exposure and resist extinction. Öhman & Mineka.
  • Fears peak developmentally when the danger was most relevant (e.g. fear of strangers emerges ~ when infants begin to crawl).

Landscape preference

The savanna hypothesis: people across cultures prefer scenes with features of the African savanna — open grassland with scattered trees, water, and signs of game. Trees people like most have the low, spreading canopy of savanna acacias (refuge + prospect).

04 Long-Term Mating

Where sex differences are sharpest. Parental investment asymmetry predicts who competes and who chooses — and for what.

Because a woman's minimum obligate investment (gestation, lactation) far exceeds a man's, ancestral women faced steeper reproductive costs and evolved to be the choosier sex. Both sexes invest heavily in long-term mating, so both are choosy here — but they weight different qualities.

What men's attraction tracks

Cues that ancestrally correlated with fertility and reproductive value: youth, clear skin, symmetry, and a waist-to-hip ratio near 0.7 (Singh) — a cue to health, hormonal profile, and not currently pregnant. Standards of weight vary by culture and food scarcity, but the WHR signal is remarkably consistent.

What women's attraction tracks

Cues to ability and willingness to invest: resources, status, ambition, slightly older age, plus good genes markers (symmetry, masculinity, health) and, critically, signals of commitment — love is read as a hard-to-fake sign that investment will be directed to her and her children.

The landmark evidence is Buss's 37 Cultures study (1989), n ≈ 10,000 across 6 continents — the first large cross-cultural test of these predictions. See the dedicated visualization →

05 Short-Term Mating

Sexual Strategies Theory: both sexes pursue short- and long-term mating, but the adaptive problems — and payoffs — differ.

Buss & Schmitt, 1993 — Sexual Strategies Theory.

Male short-term

Adaptive problem: partner number

Reproductive payoff scaled with number of fertile partners. Predicted psychology: desire for variety, larger desired partner counts, lower minimum standards for a fling, and faster consent to sex.

Female short-term

Adaptive problem: which benefits?

Extra offspring don't follow from extra partners, so short-term mating must pay another way: resources/gifts, mate insurance/switching, or good genes. Choosiness stays high.

Both sexes

Context-sensitive strategies

Everyone has the whole menu; which strategy activates depends on sex ratio, own mate value, relationship status, and cues in the environment.

The "good genes" / ovulation angle

Some evidence that women's mating psychology shifts across the ovulatory cycle — near ovulation, relatively greater attraction to symmetry and masculine cues (the dual-mating hypothesis). Note: several of these effects have been contested in later replications, so treat as suggestive rather than settled.

06 Conflict Between the Sexes

Different mating strategies create predictable friction — deception, jealousy, and mate guarding.

Sexual overperception bias

A textbook application of Error Management Theory. For ancestral men, missing a real mating opportunity was costlier than a wasted approach, so selection favored a bias to over-infer sexual interest from ambiguous female cues. Women show the mirror-image commitment skepticism bias — under-inferring men's commitment, since falsely trusting a low-investing man was very costly.

Jealousy — the classic sex difference

Because fertilization is internal, ancestral men (unlike women) faced paternity uncertainty — the risk of investing in another man's child. Women always know the child is theirs but risk losing a mate's investment to a rival.

Buss, Larsen, Westen & Semmelroth, 1992 — forced-choice & physiological data. The size and interpretation of the effect are debated, but the qualitative sex difference replicates widely.

Mate retention & guarding

Tactics range from vigilance and concealment (monopolizing time, guarding) to benefit-provisioning (gifts, affection, resource display). Men's guarding tends to track partner youth/attractiveness; women's tends to track partner income/status — mirroring what each sex most values and can least afford to lose.

07 Parenting & Kinship

Investment follows genetic relatedness and expected reproductive return — with some sharp, testable asymmetries.

Genetic relatedness shapes investment

  • Cinderella effect — children are dramatically more at risk of abuse and homicide from a stepparent than a genetic parent; stepparents invest less on average. Daly & Wilson.
  • Paternity uncertainty gradient in grandparents: maternal grandmother invests most (two certain links), paternal grandfather least (two uncertain links); the two mixed grandparents fall in between.
  • Trivers–Willard — parents in good condition may bias investment toward sons (who can out-reproduce if they thrive); parents in poor condition toward daughters (safer reproductive floor).

Parent–offspring conflict

Trivers, 1974. Parent and child are only 50% related, so their genetic interests overlap but don't coincide. The child is selected to demand more investment than the parent is selected to give — weaning conflict, sibling rivalry, and tantrums are predicted consequences.

Kin selection in action

Altruism is steeply graded by relatedness and by the recipient's reproductive value: help flows most to close kin, and (among kin) more toward those with high remaining reproductive potential. Life-or-death help is especially kin-biased. Burnstein, Crandall & Kitayama.

08 Problems of Group Living

Cooperation, status, and aggression — the adaptive problems of living among others.

Reciprocal altruism & cheater detection

Sustained cooperation among non-kin requires spotting free-riders. On the Wason selection task, people reason poorly with abstract logic but sharply when the same problem is framed as detecting a cheater in a social contract — evidence of a specialized cheater-detection mechanism. Cosmides & Tooby.

Status & hierarchy

Two routes to rank: dominance (coercion, intimidation) and prestige (freely conferred respect for skill/knowledge). Higher status ancestrally converted into resources and, especially for men, mating access — so status striving is predicted to be more intense in men.

Aggression & warfare

  • Aggression is strategic, not random — deployed to co-opt resources, defend against attack, inflict costs on rivals, deter mates' infidelity, and negotiate status.
  • Homicide data: perpetrators and victims are overwhelmingly young men killing young men — a signature of intrasexual competition. Daly & Wilson, Homicide.
  • Women more often use indirect aggression (reputation attack, exclusion) — lower physical risk, protecting their higher parental investment.

09 Quick Glossary

Fast definitions for the terms that recur most.

TermMeaning
