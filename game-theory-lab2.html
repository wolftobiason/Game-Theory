The Key Insight

Fairness Beats Rationality

A fully rational agent should accept any offer above $0 — even $1 beats nothing. But humans (and your opponents) have fairness instincts. We're wired to reject "insulting" offers even at personal cost. This is called inequity aversion and it's been found in every human culture studied — and even in some primates.

🌐 Public Goods
How it works

Each round, all 4 players start with 10 tokens. Choose how many to contribute to a shared pool. The pool is multiplied by 2, then split equally. Contribute more = bigger pot for everyone. Contribute less = pocket tokens but free-ride on others' generosity.

The math

If all 4 contribute 10 → pool = 40 → ×2 = 80 → ÷4 = 20 each

Full cooperation pays everyone double. But if you contribute 0 while others contribute 10, you pocket 10 + 20 = 30. The temptation to defect is real.

The Key Insight

Tragedy of the Commons

The Nash equilibrium is zero contribution — defecting dominates regardless of what others do. But the social optimum is full contribution. This gap explains overfishing, carbon emissions, vaccine hesitancy, and every other "commons" problem. The solution isn't just moral — it requires reputation systems, punishment mechanisms, or repeated interaction.

The Key Insight

Iterated Reasoning & Market Bubbles

The Nash equilibrium is 0 — if everyone reasons infinitely, they all converge to zero. But real experiments find averages of 22–35, meaning most people think 1–2 levels deep. This is exactly why financial bubbles form: markets aren't populated by infinitely rational actors — they're populated by people guessing what other people will guess. Keynes called it "picking the face the judges will prefer, not the face you find prettiest."

The Twist

This is a normal auction except: both the winner and the runner-up must pay their final bid. Only the winner gets the $10. The auction starts at $1. You go first. Bids increase by $1 each turn.

The Trap

Once you've bid $4 and your opponent bids $5, if you fold you lose $4. But if you bid $6 and win, you spend $6 to win $10 — still ahead. So you bid $6. Then they bid $7... Once started, it's nearly impossible to stop rationally.

The Key Insight

Sunk Cost & Escalation of Commitment

The rational choice is to never enter the auction — the expected outcome is that both players lose money. But once you've bid, the sunk cost makes folding feel like "throwing away" what you paid. Each new bid seems like it might "rescue" the previous one. This pattern explains arms races, corporate price wars, prolonged litigation, and why countries keep fighting losing wars. The dollar auction was invented by economist Martin Shubik specifically to demonstrate this trap.
