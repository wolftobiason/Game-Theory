The Two Axes

"Capitalism vs socialism" is usually drawn as one line. It is actually a plane: how free are the markets is one axis, how big is the state is the other. Put the world's major economies on that plane and the familiar labels fall apart: the "socialist" Nordics sit on the free-market side, and the fight turns out to be about something else entirely: who pays, and for what.

01 · The Broken Ruler

One line can't hold these countries

The textbook definition is about ownership of the means of production: under socialism the state owns and directs production; under capitalism private actors do, coordinated by prices. By that definition almost every country below is capitalist. But the one-line version forces absurd placements. Where does Denmark go, with easier firing rules than the US and a bigger welfare state than France? Where does Singapore go, with the world's freest markets and a state that builds 80% of its housing?

The fix is to split the line into two questions that turn out to be independent: (1) how freely do markets operate: trade, prices, regulation, property rights; and (2) how much of the economy flows through the state: taxes, transfers, public services. A country can score high on both, low on both, or split them. That gives four corners, not two ends.

Under the hood: two free economies, opposite recipes

One freedom score compresses a dozen dials, and the compression hides the best story on the map. The Heritage Foundation's 2025 index breaks it apart for the US (70.2, ranked 26th) and Denmark (79.1, ranked 7th). The US wins exactly where the state is small: lighter taxes, less spending. Denmark wins nearly everywhere markets actually operate: starting and running a business, trading across borders, foreign investment, and a cleaner rule of law. And the single worst score in either column belongs to the US: a flat 0.0 on fiscal health. Public debt is 118.7% of GDP against Denmark's 29.7%. The smaller American state is bought with borrowed money; the bigger Danish one is paid for in full.

One honest caveat: the map's x-axis uses Fraser's index, which puts the US a hair above Denmark; Heritage puts Denmark nine points ahead. The disagreement is mostly weighting. Fraser rewards a small state as freedom per se; Heritage also asks whether the books balance. Where the two agree completely is the shape: America's freedom is a small, indebted state with middling openness; Denmark's is a big, solvent state wrapped around some of the openest markets on earth.

03 · Case Study

Sweden ran the experiment so you don't have to

Bernie Sanders' favorite example of socialism that works is Scandinavia. The Cato Institute's favorite rebuttal, by the Swedish historian Johan Norberg, is that Sweden got rich under radical free markets, nearly wrecked itself in its one genuinely socialist-leaning stretch (roughly 1970-1990), and then re-liberalized harder than almost anyone. The timeline below is Norberg's account; the caveats section afterward is where his framing gets pushback. Both are worth holding at once.

1850s–1860s
Finance minister Johan August Gripenstedt, a Bastiat devotee, abolishes the guilds, tears down trade barriers, deregulates business. He is mocked as a coward who fled office before the wreckage. Instead:
1870–1913
GDP per capita grows 2% a year, about 50% faster than the rest of Western Europe, with public spending under a tenth of GDP. A poor backwater industrializes.
1950
4th richest country in the world, 5th freest economy. Taxes are just 21% of GDP, lower than in the United States at the time. The Social Democrats (in power since 1932) govern as pragmatic free-traders who redistribute part of the surplus.
1960–1980
The socialist experiment. Public spending more than doubles, 31% → 60% of GDP. Detailed labor-market regulation; "wage earners' funds" designed to gradually socialize company ownership. IKEA relocates to the Netherlands, Tetra Pak to Switzerland, Bergman and Borg leave.
1970 → 1995
Sweden falls from 10% richer than the G7 average to 10% poorer. Over the whole period the private sector creates zero net jobs. The share of Swedes saying it's acceptable to lie to get public benefits goes from 5% (1960) to 43% (2000). Crisis in 1990-92: an 11%-of-GDP deficit and, briefly, a 500% central-bank interest rate.
1991–2006
The counter-revolution, run by right and left. Government cut by a third; wealth, property, gift and inheritance taxes abolished; state firms privatized; a universal school voucher system; social security converted to defined-contribution with private accounts. Between 1980 and 2000 Sweden gains 2 full points on the Economic Freedom index, vs 0.5 for Reagan's America.
1995 →
Real wages rise 65% after 25 years of inflation eating nearly all gains. Sweden today: big welfare state, free markets underneath it. Polls find only ~9% of Swedes call themselves socialists, a lower share than among US Republicans (15%).

"The Social Democrats' success formula is socialist rhetoric but center-right policies."

Björn Rosengren, Social Democratic minister of industry, via Norberg / Cato Policy Report 2020
04 · Who Pays

The part nobody's slogan survives

Here is the counterintuitive core, and it cuts against a common assumption (including one I'd have guessed): the US income tax code is more progressive than the Nordic ones. The Nordics don't fund their welfare states by soaking the rich. There aren't enough rich people, and the 1970s taught them that capital flees. Instead they tax everyone, heavily and flatly: roughly a third of every paycheck in payroll and municipal taxes, then a 25% VAT on nearly everything bought. In Sweden, 97% of income-tax revenue comes from those flat broad taxes; only ~3% comes from taxing "the rich" as a class. The redistribution happens on the spending side: everyone pays in big, and everyone gets healthcare, childcare and college out. The US does the reverse: steeply progressive taxes, but a much smaller pot.

05 · The Tradeoffs

What you actually choose between

Once the two axes are separated, the real tradeoffs stop being "freedom vs fairness" and become much more specific. Six of them, with the caveats the Cato framing tends to skip.

T1 · Funding

Progressive taxes vs broad taxes

A steeply progressive code (US) is politically satisfying but revenue-limited: the base is narrow and mobile. Broad flat-ish taxes plus VAT (Nordics) raise enormous, stable revenue, at the cost of being regressive on the tax side. The size of your welfare state is set by how much you're willing to tax the middle class, not the rich.

T2 · Compensation

Cash in hand vs services in kind

The American worker keeps more of the paycheck and shops for healthcare, childcare and college in markets: higher mean, much higher variance. The Nordic worker pre-pays for all three through taxes: lower disposable income, near-zero catastrophic risk. This is an insurance premium, and whether it's "worth it" depends on your risk aversion and your luck.

T3 · The Frontier

Equality vs dynamism (weaker than advertised)

Among rich democracies the growth-equality tradeoff looks surprisingly weak: the Nordics are rich and equal, and a kid born poor in Denmark climbs the income ladder more easily than one born poor in the US (intergenerational elasticity ~0.15 vs ~0.45 in the Chetty/Corak literature).

But the frontier still tilts American: the trillion-dollar companies, the top-end wages and most of the breakthrough R&D come out of the big, unequal, high-variance market. Sweden consumes technologies that Silicon Valley's incentive structure produced.

T4 · Two Freedoms

Free markets ≠ free politics

The color and the position of each bubble are separate facts. Singapore pairs the world's freest economy with dominant-party soft authoritarianism; China runs markets under one-party control; Norway pairs the world's strongest democracy with a state that owns a $1.8T oil fund. Neither axis buys the other automatically, though the poorest performers tend to be unfree on both.

T5 · Preconditions

Trust is the load-bearing wall

The Nordic model was built in small, homogeneous, extremely high-trust societies where free-riding was socially policed. Even there it eroded: Gunnar Myrdal, the model's own architect, despaired at the "population of cheats" the benefit system trained. Whether the machinery transplants to a country of 330 million with low institutional trust is the genuinely open question, and both sides of the US debate answer it with vibes.

T6 · Labor

Protect the job vs protect the worker

Italy and France make firing hard: insiders are safe, outsiders (the young) queue for years. Denmark's "flexicurity" makes firing easy and unemployment generous with aggressive retraining: protection attaches to the person, not the position. Notably, Denmark and Sweden have no statutory minimum wage at all; unions negotiate sector floors instead. Security and flexibility are not opposites if you're willing to redesign where the safety net hangs.

"Denmark is far from a socialist planned economy. Denmark is a market economy."

Lars Løkke Rasmussen, Danish Prime Minister, Harvard Kennedy School, 2015
