The Reputation Economy

Based on Nowak & Sigmund's 1998 paper on how image scores, gossip, and indirect reciprocity explain large-scale human cooperation without requiring you to ever meet the same person twice.

⚡ Two Roads to Cooperation

Axelrod showed trust can evolve between two people who keep meeting. Nowak & Sigmund show how it can evolve across an entire society.

Axelrod (1981) - Direct Reciprocity

  • Mechanism: You help those who helped you
  • Memory: Personal - your history with that person
  • Scale: Pairs, small stable groups
  • Requirement: Repeated encounters with the same individual
  • Condition: w > c/b (shadow of the future)
  • Winner: Tit-for-Tat

Nowak & Sigmund (1998) - Indirect Reciprocity

  • Mechanism: You help those who helped others
  • Memory: Public - the whole community's image scores
  • Scale: Large anonymous societies
  • Requirement: Only that reputation information flows
  • Condition: q > c/b (gossip quality)
  • Winner: Discriminator (k = 0)

The key insight: You don't need to ever interact with the same person again. As long as you can observe - or hear about - how they've treated others, that's enough for cooperation to evolve. This is why language, gossip, reviews, and reputation systems are among the most powerful cooperative technologies humans have ever developed.

The recipient's score never changes - only the donor's. Refusing to help someone is a public signal. Helping someone is a public signal. Everyone in the community can see your running total.

The k-Threshold Strategy

Each player follows one simple rule: donate if the recipient's image score is at least k.

cooperate → (score not known) OR (recipient.imageScore ≥ k)

When you don't know someone's reputation (probability 1 − q), you cooperate by default - giving the benefit of the doubt. When you do know, you apply your threshold. The simulation tracks what happens across many rounds as strategies with different k values compete for survival.

🔑 The Key Condition: q > c/b

Cooperation through indirect reciprocity can evolve if and only if the quality of reputation information exceeds the cost-to-benefit ratio of helping.

0.70

High q = tight-knit community, reliable gossip, visible reputation. Low q = large anonymous population, information doesn't travel.

0.33

Low c/b = cheap to help, big benefit (e.g. giving directions). High c/b = costly help relative to gain (e.g. large sacrifice).

For indirect reciprocity to evolve:
q > c/b

The analogy to Hamilton's Rule: In kin selection, relatedness (r) replaces q. You help relatives because r > c/b. Nowak & Sigmund show that reputation tracking is the functional equivalent of relatedness - "reputational acquaintanceship" plays the role that genes play in kin selection.

🌍 Reputation in the Real World

The math maps directly onto human institutions. Everywhere you see reputation infrastructure, you're looking at a mechanism that raises q.

🗣️
Gossip is a Cooperation Technology
Gossip has a bad reputation, but the model shows it's functionally essential. Without information flow (low q), discriminators can't protect themselves from exploiters. Gossip networks, village talk, and "whisper networks" all raise q - making cooperation possible at larger scales than direct observation allows.
⭐
Review Systems are Image Score Machines
Yelp, Uber, Amazon, Airbnb, GitHub stars - these are all engineered image-score systems. They make reputation visible and sticky in anonymous markets. The model predicts that cooperation collapses in their absence (Craigslist vs. eBay is a natural experiment). The condition q > c/b holds whenever the platform scales reputation effectively.
⚖️
Law as Reputation Infrastructure
Laws, courts, credit scores, business registrations - all make defection visible and its consequences durable. They're state-level gossip systems with memory. The rule of law essentially raises q toward 1 for high-stakes transactions, which is why legal systems correlate so strongly with economic development and large-scale cooperation.
🦠
Why Cities Feel Less Cooperative
Anonymous urban environments have low q - strangers don't know your reputation. This is exactly why cities feel less cooperative than villages. It's not that city dwellers are less moral; it's that the game-theoretic condition for indirect reciprocity (q > c/b) fails in settings where reputation doesn't travel. Institutions compensate.
🎭
The Reciprocity Feigner
Cooperate visibly to build image score, then exploit the accumulated goodwill - this pattern maps directly onto predatory behavior in real markets. A company or individual can feign genuine reciprocity long enough to build trust, then cash it out through defection. The model predicts this fails long-term if q is high and memory is long. It succeeds when q is low or reputation resets.
🧬
The Bridge to Morality
Nowak & Sigmund argue that indirect reciprocity - enforced by image scores and community gossip - was "a decisive step" in human evolution. It scales cooperation beyond kin and direct acquaintance. They suggest it co-evolved with language (the gossip tool) and moral intuitions (the image-score algorithm), explaining why moral judgments feel so universally intuitive.

🧭 How to Navigate a Reputation Economy

The model implies clear strategies for building trust and protecting yourself in reputation-based environments.

  • 1

    Cooperate publicly, not just privately

    In direct reciprocity, private favors are enough. In indirect reciprocity, visibility matters - your image score only rises when others can observe your cooperation. Helping quietly is virtuous; helping visibly is strategically essential for reputation to work as a cooperative mechanism.

  • 2

    Discriminate based on reputation, not feelings

    The Discriminator wins because it applies consistent, information-based criteria - not because it's kind or harsh. In practice: check track records, ask references, read reviews. Don't let charm substitute for reputation data. And don't let personal dislike substitute for it either.

  • 3

    Know the q of your environment

    If you're in a small, tight-knit industry (law, academia, a specific tech niche), q is very high. Your reputation travels everywhere, forever. If you're in an anonymous market, q is low. Calibrate how much you invest in reputation accordingly - and be more cautious about defectors in low-q settings, since the community can't automatically punish them.

  • 4

    Beware Exploiters

    Watch for patterns like: high initial generosity, then escalating extraction. This is the Exploiter strategy in action - cashing out accumulated image score. One signal: they cooperate very visibly with powerful people (audience-building) while quietly defecting on less visible interactions.

  • 5

    Invest in reputation infrastructure around you

    In teams, communities, and companies: build systems that make cooperation and defection visible. Feedback loops, transparent accountability, peer recognition, post-mortems. These raise q for your group and make the Discriminator strategy viable for everyone - dramatically increasing collective cooperation without requiring individual vigilance.

  • 6

    Recover proactively from reputation damage

    Image scores can be rebuilt. The model shows that even agents who defect and drop below 0 can recover by cooperating consistently. In real life: public acknowledgment, sustained changed behavior, and giving even where not observed. The key is raising your score above the k threshold of the discriminators whose help you need.

The deeper truth: Direct and indirect reciprocity are two complementary pillars of cooperation. Where repeated interaction and personal memory sustain trust between individuals, reputation and image scoring extend that same logic to strangers - to cities, markets, and societies where you never meet the same person twice. The institutions humans have built - law, credit, reviews, gossip - are all, at bottom, mechanisms for tracking and broadcasting image scores. You are always being scored. You are always scoring others. The question is whether the system around you is honest enough, and information-rich enough, to make that scoring matter. - Nowak & Sigmund, Nature 1998.
