Part II — Sixty Years of Real Markets

Does the number survive?

1,000 simulated lifetimes. Each year the portfolio earns a random real return, pays California taxes on dividends and the gains in what you sell (muni coupons are tax-exempt), then funds your spending. The fan shows where the middle 80% of lives end up. This is the ergodicity problem: you only get one path.

Portfolio & Taxes

Stocks: ~6.5% real, 17% vol. Rest in CA municipal bonds: ~3.5% coupon exempt from federal & CA tax, ~1.2% real total return, 6% vol.
Default 37% ≈ CA top bracket: 20% federal LTCG + 3.8% NIIT + 13.3% CA (which taxes gains as ordinary income). Applied yearly to stock dividends and to the gains embedded in each withdrawal — muni coupons are exempt, and unrealized gains keep compounding untaxed.

Spending Path

Flexible spending. When withdrawals exceed 5% of the portfolio, cut spending 15% (floor: 60% of plan). Restore when things recover.

Part III — Walk One Life

Live it year by year

The fan chart is the ensemble. This is one draw — your actual deal. Advance through the decades and watch what sequence-of-returns risk feels like from inside. Same assumptions as above.

Tracks The Number from Part I until you move it — then it's yours.
What this is: an estimating toy, not financial advice. The model draws annual real returns from correlated normals (stocks ~N(6.5%, 17%), CA munis ~N(1.2%, 6%), ρ=0.1) — real markets have fatter tails than this, which means the bad scenarios here are, if anything, too kind. Taxes are modeled as cash flows: stock dividends (1.8% yield) are taxed every year, while muni coupons (3.5% nominal) are exempt from federal and California tax; spending beyond that income is funded by selling shares, and the embedded gain in each sale is taxed — starting near zero (fresh basis) and ramping to 70% of each sale by year 28. Unrealized gains compound untaxed, as in life. The muni sleeve ignores credit risk and single-state concentration — a real if modest simplification. Withdrawals happen at the start of each year; everything is in today's dollars. The liquid Number is found by bisection: the smallest starting portfolio whose simulated success rate (2,000–4,000 lives, common random numbers) meets the certainty target. Treat the 999/1000 setting with suspicion — extreme-tail certainty is exactly where the thin-tailed return assumption lies most. The houses are excluded from the portfolio and assumed to roughly hold real value. Danish taxation, currency risk, and citizenship logistics are real complications this page waves at but does not model.

The point of Part III: the ensemble average is not your average — you live one path, once. If the one you're walking dies at 70, it is no comfort that the median life ended rich. That asymmetry is why the flexible-spending result matters more than any other number on this page: the willingness to cut back is worth more than an extra few million up front.
