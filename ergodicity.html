Chapter I

A bet you can't refuse

A friend offers you a coin flip. Heads, you gain 50% of your wealth. Tails, you lose 40%. The coin is perfectly fair — 50/50.

Before you decide, calculate the expected value.

The Coin Flip
Heads
+50%
Tails
−40%
Expected Value = (50% × +50%) + (50% × −40%) = +5% per flip

The math is unambiguous. Every flip adds 5% to your expected wealth. If a thousand people each flip once, the average outcome is a gain. If you flip a thousand times, the average is even larger.

You'd be irrational not to take this. Right?

"Expected value is not the right tool for repeated bets when ruin is possible." — Nassim Nicholas Taleb, Skin in the Game

The ensemble mean is extraordinary. A handful of lucky survivors made astronomical gains and dragged the average skyward. This is the number your brain reaches for when it hears "expected value."

But the median player — the typical outcome — is close to zero. More than half the players end up worse off than when they started. Many are completely ruined.

The expected value was real. It just described a population, not a person. You only get one life — one path through time. The ensemble doesn't apply to you.

Chapter IV

Two kinds of average

There is no contradiction here — only a confusion between two different quantities that both go by the name "average."

Arithmetic mean per flip ×1.05  →  +5% per flip
Calculation 0.5 × 1.5  +  0.5 × 0.6  =  1.05

The arithmetic mean describes what happens across people in a single round. If 1,000 people each flip once, the average multiplier is 1.05. This is the ensemble average — and it's real, as long as you're talking about a population.

Geometric mean per flip ×0.949  →  −5% per flip
Calculation √(1.5 × 0.6)  =  √0.9  ≈  0.949

The geometric mean describes what happens to one person over time. Each flip multiplies your wealth. Multiplications chain together — and the long-run growth rate is the geometric mean, which is negative. You lose about 5% per flip, on average, over time.

Expected wealth after 100 flips $131,501
Typical player after 100 flips $5
The divergence 26,300×

This process is non-ergodic: the time average (one player over many flips) is not equal to the ensemble average (many players over one flip). Standard expected value assumes ergodicity. Most of real life violates it.

"The first principle is that you must not fool yourself — and you are the easiest person to fool. Expected value, in non-ergodic situations, will fool you." — Nassim Nicholas Taleb, paraphrased from The Technical Incerto

Once you're ruined, you cannot play again. Zero is an absorbing barrier — no subsequent win can rescue you. This is why the variance kills you even when the mean is positive. You only need to be ruined once.

Chapter V

The fix: Kelly sizing

The bet isn't broken. Your position size is. What if, instead of betting your entire wealth each flip, you only risked a fraction of it?

The Kelly Criterion gives you the mathematically optimal fraction to bet to maximize long-run growth without risking ruin. For this specific bet, the answer is 25% of your wealth per flip.

Kelly fraction (f*) (p × b − q) / b  →  25%
With Kelly: Heads 1 + (0.5 × 0.25) = ×1.125
With Kelly: Tails 1 − (0.4 × 0.25) = ×0.900
Geometric mean per flip √(1.125 × 0.90) ≈ ×1.006  →  +0.6% per flip

Smaller gain per flip — but the geometric mean is now positive. You grow steadily over time rather than drifting toward zero. See the difference:

Chapter VI

What ergodicity means for your life

This isn't a coin flip abstraction. Non-ergodicity is the structure of almost every consequential decision you make.

Fragile

Maximizing expected return while ignoring ruin. Investing 100% in volatile assets. Betting everything on a single company. One bad outcome ends the game.

Robust

Survive first, grow second. Never risk so much that a single loss means you can't play again. Kelly sizing. Diversification. Keeping the game going.

Antifragile

Barbell strategy: safe core + asymmetric upside bets. Small, capped-loss positions with unlimited upside. Gain from volatility rather than being destroyed by it.

"In a strategy that entails ruin, benefits never offset risks of ruin. Never cross a river if it is on average four feet deep." — Nassim Nicholas Taleb, Antifragile

The classical economist asks: "What is the expected value?" Taleb asks: "Can I survive long enough to see the expected value?" These are completely different questions, and only one of them applies to a single life lived through time.

Ergodicity is the gap between what works on paper and what works for you, in one lifetime, without a second chance.
