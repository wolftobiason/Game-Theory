What the research
actually shows

Every mechanism in this series rests on a claim about human behavior. This page traces each one to its evidence — and, just as important, to its limits. Some findings are robustly replicated; some are suggestive; a few are contested or partly folk wisdom. A framework is only as honest as its willingness to say which is which.

How to Read This

Three parts to every entry

The point isn't to prove the framework right — it's to show you exactly how much weight each piece can bear, so you can lean on the strong ones and hold the weak ones loosely.

The Claim

What the series asserts — the mechanism as it appears on the Ladder, the Long Game, or the Channels.

The Evidence

The study or literature behind it, and what it actually found. Numbered references below.

The Limit

Where the finding stops — replication gaps, alternative explanations, effect sizes, or plain uncertainty.

Cluster I

Status & Preselection

Preselection is real

Ref 1 · mate-choice copying
"The strongest evidence a man is desirable is that other women treat him as desirable." — The Ladder, Preselection
Evidence

Across experiments, women rate the same man as more attractive when he's shown with, or being smiled at by, another woman than when shown alone. A 2018 systematic review and meta-analysis confirms a reliable female mate-choice-copying effect. The signal is real and measurable.

Limit

Two big caveats. First, it's conditional: copying mainly occurs when the paired woman is herself attractive. Second, and more deflating, one strand of work finds the same social-influence effect for photos of hands and abstract art — suggesting it may be domain-general social learning, not a mate-specific adaptation. The effect holds; the evolutionary story behind it is not settled.

Women weight status & resources more

Ref 2 · Buss 1989, 37 cultures
"Women weight status and resource cues more heavily than men do." — The Long Game, Resource access
Evidence

Buss's survey of 10,000+ people across 37 cultures found a consistent sex difference: women rated "good financial prospect" and ambition/industriousness higher than men did, in the large majority of samples. One of the most replicated findings in the field.

Limit

It's a difference in average emphasis, not a top ranking. In the same data, both sexes rank mutual attraction–love, dependable character, emotional stability, and kindness above status and money. The status finding is robust but secondary — a page that treats status as the main event overreads it. (This is why the framework puts valence over reach.)

Cluster II

The Vetting Biases

Commitment skepticism

Ref 3 · Haselton & Buss 2000
"Her commitment skepticism is evolved and rational — it ignores claims and reads structure." — The Transience Discount
Evidence

Error Management Theory predicts, and Haselton & Buss found, that women systematically underestimate men's commitment intent. The logic is asymmetric cost: ancestrally, over-trusting a low-commitment man risked pregnancy without support, so a skeptical bias was protective. This directly underwrites the "why invest in a guy who's leaving" objection as rational pricing, not an excuse.

Limit

Robust as a lab finding, but it's a bias about reading intent, not a proven formula for what dissolves it. The framework's claim that verifiable structure beats verbal claims is a reasonable extension of the theory, not a directly tested result. Treat "prove, don't promise" as well-motivated, not laboratory-certified.

Competitor derogation

Ref 4 · Buss & Dedden 1990
"A clean record is armor against an attack people actually deploy." — The Vouch, Negativity Veto
Evidence

Buss & Dedden documented that people derogate rivals precisely on the dimensions the opposite sex values — rivals' reputation, fidelity, and appearance are common targets. Rival-badmouthing is a real, patterned tactic, which is what makes a spotless record defensively valuable.

Limit

The study shows the tactic exists and is patterned; it doesn't quantify how often derogation actually changes a mate decision. The "one bad report vetoes years of good ones" asymmetry is consistent with negativity-bias research broadly, but the specific veto strength is an inference, not a measured coefficient.

Cluster III

Networks & Reputation

Weak ties & structural holes

Ref 5 · Granovetter 1973 · Burt 1992
"The bridge into a circle you can't reach is the highest-value tie." — The Option Book, network tie
Evidence

Granovetter's "strength of weak ties" showed novel opportunities (famously, jobs) flow disproportionately through weak, bridging connections, not close ones — because distant ties carry non-redundant information. Burt's "structural holes" formalized the value of being the bridge between otherwise-disconnected groups.

Limit

This is sociology of jobs and information, not dating. Applying it to romantic introductions is a well-reasoned analogy — introductions plausibly behave like other opportunities — but it's borrowed, not a mating-specific result. Strong theory, adjacent domain.

Reputation / image scoring

Ref 6 · Nowak & Sigmund 1998
"Cooperation based on a reputation score, not direct experience." — the vouch as indirect reciprocity
Evidence

Nowak & Sigmund's indirect-reciprocity models show cooperation can evolve when individuals act on others' reputation ("image score") rather than personal history. Formally, this is exactly what a vouch is: a third party's score standing in for firsthand knowledge. (See your own indirect-reciprocity page.)

Limit

These are mathematical/evolutionary models and cooperation-game experiments, not studies of human courtship. They establish that reputation-based decisions are a coherent, evolvable strategy — they don't measure how much a vouch actually moves a real dating outcome.

Cluster IV

Reading People

Thin slices carry real signal

Ref 7 · Ambady & Rosenthal 1992
"Thin-slice judgment is reliable for warmth and intelligence." — The Read
Evidence

Ambady & Rosenthal's meta-analysis showed that judgments from very short observations (seconds to minutes) predict outcomes surprisingly well for some traits — especially expressive, interpersonal ones. First impressions are not noise.

Limit

Accuracy is trait-dependent. Thin slices do well on extraversion and warmth, poorly on traits people actively conceal or that require behavioral history. "I can read intelligence and kindness fast" is defensible; "I can read anything fast" is not.

The narcissism asymmetry

Ref 8 · Back, Schmukle & Egloff 2010
"Trust the alarm, distrust the silence, let time break the tie." — The Read, screening rule
Evidence

Narcissists make more favorable first impressions — but the effect traces to stylish appearance and expressive charm, not likeable character, and popularity declines as acquaintances deepen (confirmed in longitudinal peer-group work). This is exactly the "charm masks it early, time reveals it" structure the gate encodes.

Limit

The research supports "time reveals narcissism" and "early charm can mask it." It does not validate the stronger personal claim that a sensed hit is always a true positive — that's a calibration statement about one individual's detector, which no study can certify. The directional rule (a hit warrants caution; a clean read isn't safety) is well-supported; the certainty of any given hit is not.

Trusting the intuitive danger read

Ref 9 · de Becker 1997 (popular)
"Don't argue yourself out of a real red flag." — The Read, Stage 1
Evidence

de Becker's The Gift of Fear argues intuition often registers danger before conscious reasoning does, and that people frequently override a correct read for social reasons. Widely influential and intuitively resonant.

Limit

This one is the softest. It's a practitioner's book, not peer-reviewed research, and it selects on dramatic cases. Intuition is also demonstrably biased (it encodes prejudice as readily as insight). Use it as a prompt to take a real unease seriously — never as proof that a feeling is correct.

The Naturalistic Fallacy

One warning that governs the whole series. Everything here describes what evolved tendencies are — not what anyone ought to value. That a bias was ancestrally adaptive says nothing about whether acting on it is wise or good today; "natural" is not a synonym for "right." Buss himself is careful about this line, framing his work as description, not prescription.

Read the framework the same way: it's a map of the terrain, useful for understanding why a market behaves as it does — not a license, and not an instruction to reduce people to the variables that happen to be measurable. The most-cited traits in the data, remember, are kindness, dependability, and love — the parts hardest to game.
